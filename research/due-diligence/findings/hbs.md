## 0.1 How top firms run a diligence process

- Buyout deal funnel benchmark: for every 100 opportunities considered, the average PE firm deeply investigates fewer than 24, signs an LOI/agreement with fewer than 14, and closes about 6. A sponsor that claims to close a much larger share of what it looks at is either not screening or not seeing competitive deal flow. [NEW] (Gompers, Kaplan, Mukharlyamov, "What Do Private Equity Firms Say They Do?", NBER WP 21133 / JFE 2016, https://www.nber.org/papers/w21133)
- Venture deal funnel benchmark: per closed deal a VC firm considers about 100 opportunities (151 in IT, 78 in healthcare); about 1 in 4 get a management meeting, a third of those reach a partners meeting, half of those go to due diligence, and a third of diligenced deals get a term sheet. Firms issue 1.7 term sheets per closed deal (about 60% close rate). [NEW] (Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan, Strebulaev, "How Do Venture Capitalists Make Decisions?", JFE 2020 / NBER WP 22587, https://www.nber.org/papers/w22587)
- Venture diligence effort benchmark: the average VC deal takes 83 days from first contact to close, consumes about 118 hours of diligence, and involves about 10 reference calls (13 for late-stage firms, 8 for early-stage). Use these as a floor: a lead investor that spent a fraction of this is not a diligence anchor you can rely on. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.2 reference calls] (Gompers et al., How Do VCs Make Decisions?, https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/bhagat/vc-decisions.pdf)
- Sourcing reality check: PE firms say about 48% of closed deals are "proprietary" in some sense (36% self-generated, 7.4% from management, 8.6% executive network) but 33% come from investment banks, 8.6% from brokers, 4.3% from other PE firms. VC: >30% from professional networks, ~30% self-generated, ~20% other investors, 10% inbound from management, 8% portfolio referrals. Treat a "proprietary deal" claim as an assertion to verify: ask who else saw it and whether a banker ran a process. [NEW] (Gompers et al. 2016; Gompers et al. 2020, HLS Forum summary https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2019/08/20/how-do-venture-capitalists-make-decisions/)

## 0.6 Valuation methods across assets

- How practitioners actually value buyouts: PE firms rely on gross IRR and MOIC; over 70% also use comparable-company multiples; fewer than 20% use DCF/APV; at least 55% do not use WACC at all. About 96% use a 5-year forecast horizon; exit value is set by comparable multiples (fewer than 30% use a growing perpetuity; 16% simply assume exit multiple = entry multiple). [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.6] (Gompers, Kaplan, Mukharlyamov, NBER WP 21133, https://www.nber.org/papers/w21133)
- Haircut management forecasts: the median and mean PE discount to management's EBITDA forecast is 20%. Bake an explicit haircut into the base case and show it in the IC memo. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.7 item 5] (same)
- Target-return benchmarks (buyout): target gross IRR averages 22% (most 20% to 25%; median 25% in the survey table; smaller firms higher); target MOIC median 2.5x, mean 2.85x; net IRR marketed to LPs mostly 20% to 25% net (25% to 30% gross). Over 85% adjust the target IRR for risk. [NEW] (same)
- Target-return benchmarks (venture): average required IRR 31%; required MOIC mean 5.5x, median 5.0x, higher for early stage and smaller funds; VCs market about 24% net IRR (median 20%) and 3.5x net MOIC (3.8x early, 2.8x late). 64% adjust targets for risk; 9% use no quantitative metric at all and 20% do not forecast cash flows. MOIC is used by 63%, IRR by 42%. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.6 Venture row] (Gompers et al. 2020, https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/bhagat/vc-decisions.pdf)
- Exit-multiple discipline: since most sponsors set exit value off comparables, require the memo to show the exit multiple versus entry, and a case at entry minus 1 to 2 turns. "Exit = entry" is the survey's most common simplification, not a conservative one if entry was at a cyclical peak. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.7 item 6] (Gompers et al. 2016)

## 2 Tech and Venture

- What VCs weight: team is rated important by 95% and most important by 47% of VCs; business model 83%; product 74%; market 68%; industry 31%; valuation ranks fifth. Team is also cited as the top factor in both successes (96%) and failures (92%). Implication for diligence: the founder workstream (references, background, prior outcomes) deserves the most hours, not the least. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 2.3 TEAM] (Gompers et al. 2020, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2019/08/20/how-do-venture-capitalists-make-decisions/)
- Term-sheet negotiability map: VCs are least flexible (in order) on pro rata rights, liquidation preference, anti-dilution, valuation, board control and vesting; more flexible on option pool, participation, investment amount, redemption rights and dividends. Use this to read a term sheet: a lead that concedes on preference or board control but fights on dividends is atypical. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 2.6] (Gompers et al. 2020)

## 5 Private Equity and M&A

- Leverage benchmark from the survey (2012 market): median target debt/total capital 60% and debt/EBITDA 4.0x (vs 70% and 5.2x in Axelson et al. 2013 data). Leverage is set as much by credit-market conditions ("as much as the market will bear", cited by about two-thirds) as by industry trade-off; fewer than 10% cite financial flexibility. CHECK: ask the sponsor to justify leverage by the target's cash-flow volatility, not by what lenders will provide. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.4] (Gompers, Kaplan, Mukharlyamov, https://www.nber.org/papers/w21133)
- Management equity benchmark: PE firms allocate on average 17% of equity to management and employees, with about 8% to the CEO (15% and 6% at larger firms), versus about 1.6% median CEO ownership in public companies. Board: over 90% of portfolio boards have 5 to 7 members, PE holds about 3 seats, management 1 to 2, outsiders 1 to 2. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.6 MIP] (same)
- Management replacement base rates: about 31% of PE firms recruit their own senior team before investing; about 50% end up recruiting their own team after; 58% at some point. Underwrite a CEO change as a base-case possibility and diligence the bench. [NEW] (same)
- Value-creation sources (pre-deal, share of deals where important): revenue growth >70%; incentives 61%; governance 47%; follow-on acquisitions >50%; buying cheap 44% to 47%; facilitating high-value exit about 50%; cost reduction only 36% (47% post-deal). Use in the IC memo return attribution (0.7 item 6): if the plan relies on cost cuts or multiple arbitrage, the sponsor is relying on its weaker levers by its own industry's account. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.7, 5.1] (same)
- Exit route base rates: PE investors expect about 50% of exits to strategics, about 30% to financial buyers, fewer than 20% via IPO (26% for large firms, 11% for small), matching realized exits (53/30/17, Stromberg 2008). An IPO-dependent exit case is a minority outcome. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.1] (same)
- Serial-founder base rates (VC-backed companies, 1986 to 2003, success = IPO or IPO filing): founders with a prior success succeed about 30% of the time, versus 20% for founders with a prior failure and 18% for first-time founders. Top-tier VC backing raises success odds only for first-time and previously failed founders. Verify the founder's prior outcome from filings and cap tables, not the founder's bio: "prior exit" is a material underwriting variable and is often inflated. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.2, 2.3 TEAM] (Gompers, Kovner, Lerner, Scharfstein, "Performance Persistence in Entrepreneurship", JFE 2010; HLS Forum summary https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2010/04/16/performance-persistence-in-entrepreneurship-and-venture-capital/)

## 0.4 Fraud detection: the universal red flags

- Misconduct is far more frequent than enforcement data suggests. Using confidential internal records of large multinationals, Soltes finds more than two internally substantiated instances of financial misconduct per firm per week, and a misconduct case roughly every three days; internal counts run about 64 times the most expansive public database. Implication: a clean SEC/litigation search on a target is weak evidence. Ask for the internal investigations log, hotline log, and how each matter was closed. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.2, 0.4] (Soltes, "The Frequency of Corporate Misconduct: Public Enforcement versus Private Reality", Journal of Financial Crime 2019, https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3347159)
- Test the whistleblower hotline, do not just confirm it exists. In a field study placing misconduct reports with nearly 250 firms, one-fifth had a non-functional channel (disconnected phone, bounced e-mail, wrong website), and firms with predicted internal-control weaknesses or recent regulatory sanctions were more likely to discourage anonymity. Tips are the largest single detection channel (about 40% of cases per ACFE) and tip-detected frauds run a median 17 months versus 24 (auditor) and 36 (law enforcement). CHECK: call/e-mail the hotline during diligence; request hotline volume, categories, and resolution times for 3 years. [NEW] (Soltes, "The Difficulty of Being Good: The Efficacy of Integrity Hotlines", HBS 2018, https://www.stern.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/assets/documents/Eugene%20Soltes%20-%20The%20Difficulty%20of%20Being%20Good%20The%20Efficacy%20of%20Integrity%20Hotlines.pdf)
- Frauds usually start small and escalate. Madoff described a slippery slope from payment-for-order-flow practices to naked shorting, to unrecorded short sales, to a Ponzi; he "kept on waiting for the environment to change." Diligence implication: small, explained-away irregularities (a late audit, a restated quarter, an unbooked rebate) are the early-stage signal, not noise. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.4] (HBS Working Knowledge, "Bernie Madoff Explains Himself" (Soltes), https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/bernie-madoff-explains-himself)
- White-collar misconduct commonly runs about six years before exposure, typically surfacing in a downturn. A sponsor's clean record through a single bull cycle proves little. Ponzis are "psychological schemes" that recruit through trusted networks; affinity and social proof (other sophisticated investors are in) substitute for diligence. CHECK: never accept "X and Y institutions are already in" as evidence; ask what diligence those investors actually did and confirm with them directly. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.4] (HBS Working Knowledge, "AI Schemes Could Make Bernie Madoff's Fraud Look Trivial" (Soltes, 2025), https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/ai-schemes-could-make-bernie-madoffs-fraud-look-trivial-eugene-soltes)
- Emerging pattern: AI-personalized deception (synthetic voice/video of known people, tailored pitches). CHECK: verify wire instructions and any principal's instructions by call-back to a number obtained independently, never from the message. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.5] (same)

## 3 Debt to Businesses / 4 Credit (documentation weakness)

- Covenants are hollowed out by baskets and carve-outs. In 1,240 leveraged credit agreements: 92% restrict liens and 87% restrict additional debt, but 96% contain at least one deductible ("basket"), the median agreement has 8 baskets, and the average has 79 distinct carve-outs (22 on liens). The average debt basket equals more than 2.3x EBITDA; collateral and asset-sale baskets average 35% and 39% of EBITDA. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 3.6, 4.2 Direct lending] (Ivashina and Vallee, "Weak Credit Covenants", NBER WP 27316 / Management Science 2025, https://www.nber.org/papers/w27316)
- Headline leverage understates permitted leverage. About half of borrowers close below 5x total debt/EBITDA, yet over 70% of contracts permit later senior secured debt above 5x; over three-quarters of 5x borrowers can go above 6x, and a similar share of 6x borrowers can go above 7x. CHECK: for every credit, compute "maximum permitted senior secured leverage" by summing all incurrence baskets, ratio debt, and EBITDA add-backs, and put it beside closing leverage in the IC memo. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 3.4] (same)
- LBO loans and loans with more non-bank funding have significantly weaker contracts, priced only modestly higher. The market reprices the risk only after a public event (J.Crew 2016-17). [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 4.2] (same)
- Failure case: J.Crew (2016-17). Owned by TPG and Leonard Green since 2011, J.Crew used an investment basket plus a carve-out to move about $250M of brand IP out of the lenders' collateral into an unrestricted subsidiary, then used it to back new debt that refinanced junior notes; senior loan prices dropped on announcement; Chapter 11 in May 2020. CHECK: read the investments, unrestricted-subsidiary and IP-transfer provisions; require a "J.Crew blocker" (no transfer of material IP to unrestricted subs) and caps on investments in non-guarantors. [NEW] (same)
- Private credit risk map (Ivashina): growth to roughly $2 trillion since 2008 was driven by banks shedding high-LTV leveraged loans and by PE needing leverage to hit about 20% promised returns. Her main concerns are uncertain valuations (marks drive jumpy flows), high leverage and credit risk, and interconnection with banks and the rest of the system. CHECK: for any private credit fund, ask how marks are set, who independently values Level 3 loans, and what bank facilities sit beneath the fund. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 4.1] (HBS Working Knowledge, "How Private Debt Became a $2 Trillion Industry", https://library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-private-debt-became-2-trillion-industry)

## 5 Private Equity and M&A (legal, fraud claims, insurance)

- Failure case: Paragon Metals Holdings v. Smith (Del. 2026). A PE sponsor paid $100M for an auto-parts maker; the day after closing it learned two of the largest customers planned sharp volume cuts; it later defaulted on acquisition debt and injected $37M. The CEO had withheld a customer letter, stripped volume-reduction terms from a contract amendment, routed a $300K rebate through a foreign affiliate to keep it off the books, and destroyed his phone. The buyer had seen hints (diligence materials referencing volume declines, a reference to a cancellation letter) but its negotiator never asked the prepared questions in customer meetings, nobody read the CEO e-mail summarizing the letter, and the model was not updated. The Supreme Court revived the fraud claim, but only after years of litigation. CHECK: convert every hint into a written question, ask it in customer meetings, document the answer, and re-run the model on what you learned; get customer-level volume forecasts directly from top customers. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.7] (HLS Forum, "Are Hints Disclosures? Delaware Supreme Court Revives M&A Fraud Claim Despite Buyer's Red Flags", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/07/30/are-hints-disclosures-delaware-supreme-court-revives-ma-fraud-claim-despite-buyers-red-flags/)
- Anti-reliance clauses cut both ways: Delaware will not let a contract shield deliberate misrepresentations inside the agreement, and fraud claims based on diligence statements have survived non-reliance clauses in some cases. But do not rely on litigation as the remedy: diligence findings should be reflected in the price, the model, and specific indemnities. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.6] (HLS Forum, "The Looming Specter: Post-Closing Fraud Claims in Private Company M&A Litigation", 2017, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2017/07/19/the-looming-specter-post-closing-fraud-claims-in-private-company-ma-litigation/)
- Failure case: Fresenius / Akorn (2017-18). Fresenius's diligence flagged deficiencies in Akorn's testing and manufacturing but it signed anyway; post-signing whistleblower letters led to an investigation finding falsified data and FDA submissions with false data. Delaware Chancery (2018) let Fresenius walk under the MAE clause (first such ruling), but noted the agreement did not carve out diligence-disclosed matters. Akorn's CEO testified he never read the audit reports he received. CHECK: in regulated manufacturing (pharma, food, medical devices) run an independent data-integrity audit of quality systems (raw lab data vs reported data, FDA 483s and warning letters, audit trails), and draft specific closing conditions or indemnities for every known regulatory issue rather than relying on a general MAE. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.7; 11 Life sciences] (Skadden, "Analyzing Akorn", https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2018/10/analyzing-akorn; Delaware Chancery opinion, https://law.justia.com/cases/delaware/court-of-chancery/2018/ca-2018-0300-jtl.html)

## 10 Funds and Co-Investments

- Co-investments underperform. In 392 direct investments by seven large institutions (1991-2011), co-investments underperformed the fund deals they sat beside, consistent with adverse selection (GPs offer larger, weaker deals to co-investors); solo deals the LP sourced itself did better; the advantage of direct investing over funds was limited and concentrated in buyouts. CHECK: for any co-invest, ask why the GP is syndicating (size cap? concentration limit? the deal the fund would not take full?) and whether the main fund is taking its full pro rata. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.1] (Fang, Ivashina, Lerner, "The Disintermediation of Financial Markets: Direct Investing in Private Equity", JFE 2015, https://www.nber.org/papers/w19299)
- Alternative vehicles (co-invest, SMAs, sidecars) reached about 40% of PE commitments by 2017; on average they match the PE market but underperform the sponsoring GP's own main fund; top LPs get the good vehicles. If you are not a top-decile LP, assume adverse selection in what you are offered. [NEW] (Lerner, Mao, Schoar, Zhang, "Investing Outside the Box", JFE 2022, https://www.nber.org/papers/w24941)
- Re-up discipline: endowments earned materially higher PE returns than other LPs (about 14% to 21% per year higher in different versions) mainly because they were better at deciding whether to re-up with a GP's next fund; bank-selected funds lagged sharply. The skill is in re-up decisions, where the current fund's interim marks are a poor guide. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.4] (Lerner, Schoar, Wongsunwai, "Smart Institutions, Foolish Choices?", JF 2007, https://www.nber.org/papers/w11136)
- GP economics and team stability: across 717 PE partnerships, carry allocation is driven by founder status, not investment track record. The mean founder gets 19.2% of carry vs 10.9% for non-founders; mean senior partner 15% of carry and 21% of firm ownership, junior partners 7% and 3%. Underprovision of carry and ownership inequality predicts senior departures, and senior departures hurt the next fundraise. CHECK: ask for the carry and GP-ownership split by person for the current and prior fund; flag funds where those who generated the track record hold little carry, or founders hold most carry but will not be in seat for the fund's life. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.4 Alignment] (Ivashina and Lerner, "Pay Now or Pay Later?", JFE 2019, https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22660/w22660.pdf)
- NAV loans: fund-level debt secured by the portfolio, roughly $100B in 2023 with lender forecasts of $700B by 2030. Three uses: offensive (fund bolt-ons/refi), defensive (rescue struggling portfolio companies, often PIK), and liquidity (fund distributions while exits stall). Concerns: cross-collateralization contagion (good assets sold to cure LTV breach), carry paid on borrowed distributions, interim IRR/DPI inflation near a fundraise, extended fee life. CHECK: ask whether the LPA permits NAV debt and gives LPs consent; demand performance shown with and without NAV debt and sub lines; ask LTV headroom and cure plan; refuse carry on NAV-funded distributions. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.5, 10.6] (HLS Forum, "Private Equity and Net Asset Value Debt", 2024, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2024/07/15/private-equity-and-net-asset-value-loans-ticking-time-bomb-or-ticking-all-the-right-boxes/)
- Fee and expense failure cases from SEC enforcement (2015-16): (1) four affiliated PE advisers (Blackstone) accelerated monitoring fees on portfolio exits without adequate disclosure: $12.5M penalty plus $37.5M disgorgement; (2) a PE adviser allocated fee offsets among funds in a way that favored itself: $2.3M fine plus $10.4M reimbursement; (3) funds paid all insurance premiums and extra fees while the adviser negotiated a legal fee discount only for itself: $3.5M fine plus about $8.3M reimbursements; (4) unauthorized "operating partner oversight fees" and fund money used for political, charitable and entertainment expenses: $2.3M disgorgement plus penalty. CHECK: in ODD request the fee and expense allocation policy, a schedule of all portfolio-company fees (monitoring, transaction, director, operating-partner) with offset calculations, broken-deal expense allocation across funds and co-investors, and confirmation of who pays operating partners. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.3, 10.4] (HLS Forum, "The Year in Review: SEC Enforcement Actions Against Investment Advisers", 2016, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2016/12/19/the-year-in-review-sec-enforcement-actions-against-investment-advisers/; "Fund Advisers and Fee Disclosure in SEC Enforcement Action", https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2015/10/30/fund-advisers-and-fee-disclosure-in-sec-enforcement-action/)
- Fund-level LP failure-avoidance checklist derived from the cases above: (a) fee/expense allocation policy and 3-year schedule of portfolio-company fees with offsets; (b) NAV-facility and sub-line terms, plus performance ex-facilities; (c) carry/ownership split by partner; (d) co-invest allocation policy and the list of co-invests offered and to whom; (e) any SEC deficiency letters or Wells notices in the last 5 years. [NEW] (synthesis of the sources in this section)

## 5.6 Legal and structure (R&W insurance and earnouts)

- R&W insurance market terms (study of 400+ merger agreements and about 100 market participants): typical limit 10% to 20% of deal value; retention about 1% of deal value; premium about 2% to 3% of the limit; over 95% of policies buy-side; used in roughly half of private-target deals, almost never public. In insured deals any seller indemnity tends to equal the retention (about 1%), versus about 10% of deal value in uninsured deals. Policies exclude anything known to the buyer or its advisors, so whatever diligence finds is uninsured: price it or get a special indemnity. Underwriters report claim rates in the mid-to-high teens. Moral hazard concern: insured buyers may diligence less. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.6] (HLS Forum, Griffith, "Deal Insurance: Representation & Warranty Insurance in M&A Contracting", 2019, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2019/07/01/deal-insurance-representation-warranty-insurance-in-ma-contracting/)
- RWI damages can be multiplied: diminution-in-value at the purchase multiple means a $10M EBITDA overstatement at 12x can support about $120M of loss, which is why financial-statement reps dominate claim dollars. Industry claims studies (Aon, Marsh) consistently rank financial-statement breaches first by paid loss (Aon: about 38% of paid loss on 2019+ policies; Marsh 2023: 13% of claim count but nearly half of claimed dollars). The QoE is therefore both a pricing tool and the insurer's underwriting anchor. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.3 QoE] (same; Aon 2026 Transaction Solutions Global Claims Study, https://www.aon.com/insights/reports/2026-transaction-solutions-global-claims-study)
- Earnout benchmarks: outside life sciences, earnouts appear in about one in five private deals (15% in 2019, peak 30% to 37% in 2023, about 22% in 2024); median earnout is about 31% of closing payments (2024) with a median 24-month period. In life sciences, over 80% of private pharma deals use them, median about 61% of total consideration, often 3 to 5 years. Revenue is the most common metric, then EBITDA. Dispute risk rises with earnout share and length. Drafting checklist: objective metric definitions with worked examples; explicit efforts standard or fixed spend commitment; buyer reporting and audit rights; rules on cost allocation, staffing and working capital during the period; acceleration triggers; expert-not-arbitrator scope; records of efforts. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.6] (HLS Forum, "The Art and Science of Earn-Outs in M&A", 2025, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2025/07/11/the-art-and-science-of-earn-outs-in-ma/)
- Failure case: Fortis v. Johnson & Johnson (Auris Health, Del. Ch. 2024; Del. Supr. Jan 2026). Earnout milestones for a robotic-surgery product; Chancery found J&J breached its efforts obligations and fraudulently induced one milestone; the Supreme Court partly reversed, holding the negotiated "commercially reasonable efforts" definition allowed J&J to calibrate effort for the first payment but not the rest. Lesson for sellers and for anyone buying a stake whose value depends on an earnout receivable: the efforts definition is the asset; read it. [NEW] (HLS Forum, "Drafting Guidance from Delaware Supreme Court on Earnouts, Efforts Obligations, and Fraud", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/02/10/drafting-guidance-from-delaware-supreme-court-on-earnouts-efforts-obligations-and-fraud/)

## 2 Tech and Venture (additional)

- Base rates for venture outcomes (Ghosh, about 2,000 VC-backed companies that raised at least $1M, 2004-2010): about 75% never returned cash to investors; 30% to 40% liquidated with investors losing most or all; 70% to 80% missed their projected ROI; 90% to 95% missed a declared projection. Use these as priors in any VC IC memo: the base case is failure, so the memo must say what specifically makes this one different. Ghosh singles out excessive funding as the main driver of large failures (it hides problems) and irreversible early commitments made before demand is validated (Webvan). [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 2.1, 2.7] (HBS Working Knowledge, "Why Companies Fail and How Their Founders Can Bounce Back", https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/why-companies-failand-how-their-founders-can-bounce-back)
- Hot-market vintage effect: startups first funded in hot VC markets are more likely to go bankrupt but also more likely to be extreme successes, and are valued higher at IPO; true even for experienced VCs. In a hot market, widen the downside case rather than relying on the round's price as validation. [NEW] (Nanda and Rhodes-Kropf, "Investment Cycles and Startup Innovation", JFE 2013, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2011/11/28/investment-cycles-and-startup-innovation/)
- Monitoring matters, not just selection: when a new direct flight cut VC travel time to a portfolio company, the company produced more and better-cited patents and was more likely to IPO or exit successfully (about 1.0 and 1.4 percentage points). Diligence the lead investor's practical capacity to monitor (board load, distance, partner bandwidth), not just its brand. [NEW] (Bernstein, Giroud, Townsend, "The Impact of Venture Capital Monitoring", JF 2016, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/01/30/the-impact-of-venture-capital-monitoring/)
- Sahlman's four-part screen (People, Opportunity, Context, Deal) remains the standard HBS frame: on people ask "What do they know? Whom do they know? How well are they known?"; on opportunity, whether the market is large and/or growing fast and the industry structurally attractive; on context, how the team will respond when regulation, rates or competition shift; on deal, the full set of contracts (investors, channels, employees), not just the term sheet. Entrepreneurs' projections are systematically padded and investors discount them. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.7, 0.8] (HBS Working Knowledge, "How to Write a Great Business Plan" (Sahlman), https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-to-write-a-great-business-plan)
- Late-stage private company governance gap: unicorns grew from about 40 (2013) to more than 1,500; median age at IPO rose from 9 to 13 years (2012 to 2024); the JOBS Act raised the 12(g) trigger from 500 to 2,000 holders and excluded employees, removing the pressure to build public-company finance and compliance functions. FTX raised nearly $2B from VCs while having no internal audit function and no experienced finance, accounting, HR or security leadership; Theranos raised nearly $1B at a $9B valuation. Other recent convictions: Nikola (Milton), Ozy Media (Watson), Frank (Javice). CHECK (late-stage and secondaries): require named CFO and controller with public-company or audited-company experience, an audit by a firm sized for the company, internal audit or equivalent, and board composition not wholly founder-controlled; treat super-voting founder control plus no audit committee as a pricing item, not a footnote. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 2.8; 11.1] (HLS Forum, "Untamed Unicorns: Why Startup Finance is Broken and How to Fix It", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/11/04/untamed-unicorns-why-startup-finance-is-broken-and-how-to-fix-it/)

## 10 Funds and Co-Investments (fees, performance measurement)

- Fee tiers inside the same fund: across about 2,400 PE funds (1990-2019), most funds run a two-tier fee structure; within a fund, management fees differ by about 91 bps and carry by about 5.8 percentage points on average between tiers (much smaller in VC). Tier is predicted by LP size and sophistication, by the GP's law firm, and by unexplained negotiating skill and relationships; top-performing GPs use fewer tiers. CHECK: ask the GP for the full fee-tier schedule and commitment thresholds, invoke MFN, and compare your net terms to the best tier; a fee gap of 90 bps a year compounds to a large net-return gap over a 10-year fund. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.4 Fund terms] (Begenau and Siriwardane, "Fee Variation in Private Equity", JF 2024; HLS Forum summary https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2024/05/10/fee-variation-in-private-equity/)
- Pension risk allocations to alternatives rose from about 14% (2001) to almost 40% (2021) on beliefs about alpha that are hard to verify because "alternative asset classes are very hard to benchmark." CHECK: before committing, write down the expected net alpha, the benchmark it is measured against (PME vs a levered small-value public index), and how prior predictions for this GP compared with outcomes. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.5] (HBS Working Knowledge, "Rethinking Risk: Why Pension Funds Are Betting on Alternative Assets" (Siriwardane), https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/rethinking-risk-why-pension-funds-are-betting-on-alternative-assets)
- Replication benchmark (Stafford, HBS): buyout returns before fees can be largely replicated by a passive portfolio of small, low EV/EBITDA public stocks with about 2x leverage; hold-to-maturity (mark-at-cost) accounting makes that portfolio's measured beta fall from about 2 to statistically zero. After roughly 6% a year in fees, PE investors underperformed the replicating portfolio. Implications: (a) benchmark buyout GPs against a levered small-value index, not just the S&P 500 PME; (b) treat low reported volatility and low correlation as an accounting artifact of appraisal-based marks, not diversification. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.5] (Stafford, "Replicating Private Equity with Value Investing, Homemade Leverage, and Hold-to-Maturity Accounting", RFS 2022, https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/replicating-private-equity-with-value-investing-homemade-leverage-and-hold-to-maturity-accounting)
- Measured from the investor's own portfolio (1995-2018 public pensions), the average plan's risk-adjusted PE outperformance is about zero; buyouts show positive risk-adjusted performance (partly value exposure), VC underperformed on average, real estate funds showed none; pensions' better fund picks reflected access, not selection skill. Some plans earned higher PE returns only by taking more risk. [NEW] [CONTRADICTS the implicit premise that PE is an alpha source by default] (HLS Forum, "Private Equity for Pension Plans? Evaluating Private Equity Performance from an Investor's Perspective" (Korteweg, Panageas, Systla), 2025, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2025/02/03/private-equity-for-pension-plans-evaluating-private-equity-performance-from-an-investors-perspective/)
- Cost of being an LP: in present-value terms roughly half of an LP's total cost is illiquidity, a quarter management fees, a quarter carry; the break-even alpha a GP must generate is comparable to its total compensation. [NEW] (HLS Forum, Sorensen, Wang, Yang, "Valuing Private Equity", 2014, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/01/12/valuing-private-equity/)

## 6 Energy and Infrastructure

- Data center load and ratepayer/political risk: in 385 data centers across 42 utilities (2020-2025), data centers explained about 16% of industrial and 4% of residential price increases (about $5.40/MWh industrial, $1.70/MWh residential); colocation (third-party leased) centers raised consumer costs while hyperscaler-owned ones did not; utilities built for large industrial loads passed through less. LBNL projects data centers could reach up to 15% of US electricity by 2030. CHECK for any data center or power deal serving one: the large-load tariff and cost-allocation rules of the specific utility, pending rate cases, local moratorium risk, and whether the offtaker is a hyperscaler or a colocation tenant. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 6.4] (HBS Working Knowledge, "Blaming Data Centers for Higher Electric Bills? It's Not That Simple", https://library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/blaming-data-centers-for-higher-electric-bills-its-not-that-simple)
- Rate sensitivity differs by technology: clean-energy equities behave like long-duration growth assets (value falls when rates rise, because capex is upfront and payback spans decades), traditional energy the reverse; during the 525 bp Fed tightening, higher financing costs offset much of the data-center demand boost to clean energy. Worldwide data center capex rose about 51% to about $455B in 2024; data centers use about 460 TWh (about 2% of global electricity). CHECK: run every renewable project model at +200 bp on the debt and the discount rate, and show IRR sensitivity explicitly. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 6.5] (HBS AI Institute summary of Cheema-Fox, Czasonis, Kontu, Serafeim, "The AI-Energy Nexus", HBS WP 26-096, https://aiinstitute.hbs.edu/forget-the-algorithms-follow-the-electricity/)

Harvard's Gomez-Ibanez frames the core risk as the choice between regulation by contract and by discretion. CHECK: for any availability-payment or regulated-tariff asset, diligence counterparty political turnover, the tariff-adjustment mechanism's track record of actually being honored, and termination compensation. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 6.2, 6.7] (Gomez-Ibanez, Regulating Infrastructure: Monopoly, Contracts and Discretion, Harvard UP 2003, https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/regulating-infrastructure-monopoly-contracts-and-discretion; World Bank, Granting and Renegotiating Infrastructure Concessions, https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/678041468765605224/pdf/288160PAPER0Granting010renegotiating.pdf)

## 5 Private Equity and M&A (more)

- Sponsor deal pace is a measurable risk: in the largest US buyout database (thousands of targets, 1980-2013), effects differ persistently across PE groups, and rapid growth in a group's deal volume is associated with lower employment growth at its targets; productivity gains are large on average and larger for deals done in tight credit; employment falls about 12% over two years after public-to-private buyouts but rises about 15% after private-to-private buyouts. CHECK: chart the sponsor's deals per year and AUM growth vs team size; a sponsor doubling its pace without doubling its operating bench is a yellow flag for co-investors and lenders. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 0.2, 10.4] (Davis, Haltiwanger, Handley, Lipsius, Lerner, Miranda, "The (Heterogenous) Economic Effects of Private Equity Buyouts", NBER WP 26371, https://www.nber.org/papers/w26371)
- Sponsor dry powder is downside protection: in 2008-09, PE-backed companies increased investment, raised more equity and debt, grew assets and gained share versus peers, most strongly where the PE firm had more resources at the start of the crisis and the company was financially constrained. CHECK: for lenders and co-investors, confirm the sponsor's uncalled capital and reserve policy in the fund that owns the company (ability to inject equity in a downturn). [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 3.3 sponsor quality] (Bernstein, Lerner, Mezzanotti, "Private Equity and Financial Fragility during the Crisis", NBER WP 23626, https://www.nber.org/papers/w23626)
- Failure case: Cloudbreak Health / UpHealth de-SPAC (closed June 2021; Edwards v. GigAcquisitions2, Del. Ch. July 2025). Cloudbreak merged with a SPAC and five struggling health-care companies on the strength of oral assurances and management presentations prepared by the counterparty's banker; documents later showed the presentations overstated revenue and contract value and omitted insolvency and an auditor resignation; the combined company went bankrupt. The fraud claim was dismissed as time-barred: counterparties' agents owed Cloudbreak no duty, the banker's disclaimers should have prompted independent work, and Cloudbreak could have diligenced more. CHECK: when you take stock as consideration, diligence the buyer as hard as a target (own QoE, call the auditors, ask about any auditor change); get specific reps on the facts you cannot verify; diarize the fraud limitations period and indemnity survival dates. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.7, 5.8] (HLS Forum, "Chancery Decision Doubles Down on Due Diligence", 2025, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2025/08/26/chancery-decision-doubles-down-on-due-diligence/)
- Compliance diligence is now expected by prosecutors: DOJ's Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs asks whether the acquirer performed comprehensive pre-acquisition compliance diligence (and if not, why), has a process for timely integration of the target into its controls, and audits acquired entities post-close. CHECK: document compliance diligence scope and gaps in the IC memo, and put a 100-day compliance integration and audit plan in the value-creation plan. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.3 LEGAL] (HLS Forum, "Justice Department Updates Its Guidance on Corporate Compliance Programs", 2020, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2020/06/29/justice-department-updates-its-guidance-on-corporate-compliance-programs/)

## 2.7 Failure modes (Theranos detail)

- Failure case: Theranos (2003-2018). Raised close to $1B at a $9B valuation from prominent families and offices, with a board of former cabinet officials and generals but no relevant lab science expertise. Tests were run on third-party analyzers while presented as Theranos devices; investor materials carried pharma logos without authorization implying validation that never happened; investors were told the device was used on military medevac helicopters (DoD never used it); employees raised repeated internal warnings; one investor testified he invested in 2006 and 2013 with little visibility, relying on his relationship with the founder. CHECKS: (1) independent technical validation by an expert you hire, on your samples, in your presence; (2) call every named validator, partner and government user directly; (3) request error rates, complaint logs and regulator correspondence; (4) treat a celebrity board with no domain experts as a red flag; (5) refuse to invest on "relationship" without audited financials and data-room access. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 2.7, 2.8] (HLS Forum, "Theranos: The Limits of the 'Fake It Till You Make It' Strategy", 2022, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2022/01/30/theranos-the-limits-of-the-fake-it-till-you-make-it-strategy/)

## 10.3 Operational due diligence (SEC exam findings as a checklist)

- SEC examination deficiencies at private fund advisers, usable directly as ODD test steps: (1) LPAC consent skipped for conflicted transactions; (2) post-commitment-period fees not stepped down after investments were sold, written down or written off; (3) fund terms extended without required approval, keeping fees running ("zombie funds"); (4) strategy and leverage outside disclosed limits; (5) key-person events not followed or disclosed; (6) track records cherry-picked, shown gross of fees, without leverage effects, over selective periods, or borrowed from people not primarily responsible; (7) inadequate diligence on investments and on service providers (placement agents, alternative data vendors); (8) hedge clauses purporting to waive fiduciary duty except for gross negligence or fraud. CHECK: test each item against LPAC minutes, fee calculations, and the side-letter set. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.3, 10.4] (HLS Forum, "SEC Identifies Private Fund Deficiencies, Signifying Increased Industry Scrutiny", 2022, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2022/02/17/sec-identifies-private-fund-deficiencies-signifying-increased-industry-scrutiny/)
- Interim marks are weak early, GP narrative is informative early: in 7,271 company-years in 536 US PE funds (2015-2020, StepStone data), interim returns predict final returns only weakly in years 1-2 (coefficient about 0.16), rising to 0.62 in years 3-4 and 0.78 later. Meanwhile a one-standard-deviation more positive tone in early GP report commentary predicts about 3 to 4 points higher annualized final return (sample mean about 7%); commentary focused on cash flow and business model predicts better outcomes, focus on capital structure predicts worse. CHECK: in fund diligence and secondaries, read the GP's portfolio commentary history, not just the marks; flag companies where early letters dwell on refinancing, covenants or capital structure. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.4; 11 Secondaries] (HLS Forum, "Understanding Communications by Private Fund Managers", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/10/14/understanding-communications-by-private-fund-managers/)
- CEO risk base rates in buyouts: in 196 deals, 69% had at least one CEO change during the hold; a quarter of those changed more than once (2.4 transitions on average); over 60% of exit CEOs were appointed after year 2; average holds were 5.5 years with no change, 8.5 with two changes, 11.0 with four. Only 30% of replacements were internal; three-quarters of external hires came from the same sector. Top-quintile portfolio CEOs deliver about 9 points a year above peers. CHECK: run leadership diligence as a full workstream with four questions: can the incumbent execute this specific plan; can gaps be closed fast enough; who are credible internal and external successors; how will requirements change across the hold. Budget an extended hold in the downside case if a CEO change is likely. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 5.3; 0.2] (HLS Forum, "The CEO Decision: How PE Investors Select and Reassess Leaders from Entry to Exit", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/09/23/the-ceo-decision-how-pe-investors-select-and-reassess-leaders-from-entry-to-exit/)

## 5.6 Legal and structure (sponsor liability for portfolio companies)

- Control brings liability: (1) False Claims Act: DOJ named PE sponsor Riordan, Lewis & Haden alongside its portfolio pharmacy (Diabetic Care Rx) for financing a kickback scheme; settled 2019 for $21.36M; (2) ERISA multiemployer pension withdrawal liability: Sun Capital funds held to be a trade or business under common control with a bankrupt portfolio company (D. ## 2.6 Legal and structure (investor veto rights)

- Failure case: Zync v. Porsche (Del. Ch. May 2026, pleading stage). Porsche's venture arm held 5% via a $2.9M convertible note, one of three board seats, and a director-approval right over new debt. Its designee voted down a $10M Series A, Porsche withheld a promised bridge after obtaining a confidential competitor agreement, and conditioned a $50M PE offer on indemnities; the company shut down. Claims for director disloyalty, aiding and abetting, and breach of the implied covenant survived; exculpation did not cover bad faith. Lesson for investors (and for diligence of a cap table): veto rights used to harm the company create liability; directing your board designee is risky; strategic investors with vetoes over financing are a "catch-and-kill" risk for other investors. CHECK: in a venture data room, list every investor veto over financing and M&A and who holds it; treat a strategic with a financing veto as a red flag. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 2.6, 2.8] (HLS Forum, "Chancery Finds Potential Liability for Blocking Company Financings Despite Contractual Veto Rights", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/07/20/chancery-finds-potential-liability-for-blocking-company-financings-despite-contractual-veto-rights/)
- Failure case: Footprint (Guilbeau v. Footprint, Del. Ch. May 2026, pleading stage). A near-insolvent packaging company raised $500M of new Class F preferred, $450M (about 90%) from three insider funds that each had a board designee; the pre-money valuation was allegedly about half of what the funds' own earlier bridge loans implied, higher-valuation alternatives were set aside, Class A holders had three weeks to decide, and disclosures omitted the valuation, insider benefits and rejected offers. Entire-fairness review applied and aiding-and-abetting claims against the funds survived. Lesson for anyone leading or joining an insider down-round or rescue financing: price it against your own recent marks and bridges, document every rejected alternative, give non-participants real time and data access, and disclose insider benefits. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 2.6; 9.2] (HLS Forum, "Chancery Finds Funds Liable for Aiding Directors' Fiduciary Breaches", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/06/17/chancery-finds-funds-liable-for-aiding-directors-fiduciary-breaches/)

## 10.4 Diligence checklist (GP succession and LP remedies)

- GP succession: about 6% of GP leaders transition over five years (versus more than 50% turnover for public-company CEOs); fewer than half of GPs have a formal transition plan; 96% of LPs say succession readiness is decisive for re-ups. Ask: who actually makes investment decisions; who owns the LP relationship; what happens economically if a founder steps back and has carry already moved; has the successor led at scale; is governance strong enough to manage post-transition conflict. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.4 key-person] (HLS Forum, "Succession Planning in Private Equity", 2026, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/02/21/succession-planning-in-private-equity-a-strategic-imperative-for-gps-and-lps/)
- Firm break-ups following concentrated economics: Weston Presidio suspended fundraising (2014) after partners left; Doughty Hanson wound down (2015) with an investor citing concentration of power and economics at the top; Charterhouse faced friction (2015) over profit split and succession. In the Ivashina-Lerner data, senior partners who stayed held 16% carry and 23% ownership versus 9% and 13% for those who left; departures are associated with a smaller next fund. [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.6] (HBS Working Knowledge, "Is Greed Ruining Private Equity Firms?", https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/is-greed-ruining-private-equity-firms)
- Do not count on litigation as an LP remedy: of 153 lawsuits over four decades against the top and bottom 50 PE firms, only 6 (4%) were filed by fund investors and 3 (2%) by institutions; LPAs carry broad GP indemnification, exculpation, mandatory arbitration and limited information rights, and LPs fear losing allocations. A rare exception: the Abu Dhabi Investment Council sued a US PE firm in Delaware Chancery (Dec 2025) over a continuation-fund transaction. Implication: the protection you get is what you negotiate before signing (information rights, LPAC composition, no-fault removal, consent rights on GP-leds and NAV loans). [NEW] [STRENGTHENS 10.4; 10.7] (HLS Forum, "Opting Out of Court? ## 11.1 Crypto and digital assets

- Failure case: Terra/Luna (May 2022). Anchor paid a 19.5% yield on UST deposits, funded by a subsidy that reached about $6M a day by April 2022; LUNA peaked at $119.18 on April 5; the run began May 7 when two large addresses pulled 375M UST from Anchor; LUNA supply went from about 1 billion to 6 trillion in three days as UST holders swapped out, a death spiral. ## 9 Operating Company Growth Equity

- Growth equity sits inside the Gompers-Kaplan-Mukharlyamov PE survey (about 75% of respondents invest in growth equity): use the same benchmarks (20% management-forecast haircut; 22% to 25% target gross IRR; about 2.5x MOIC; exits about 50% strategic, 30% sponsor, under 20% IPO).
